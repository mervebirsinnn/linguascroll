## C1 — A Soundscape Under Strain (ns-c1-noise-habitats-v4)

Human noise from shipping, road traffic, aircraft and other industrial activity now penetrates almost every habitat on Earth, and ambient sound levels in the oceans are estimated to be around ten times higher than they were in 1950. A meta-analysis published in Science this month, which pooled 214 experimental studies of 177 aquatic and terrestrial species across six continents, concluded that noise altered at least one physiological, developmental or behavioural trait in four out of five of the species examined, with effects appearing in every type of habitat. Its authors report that predators working in noisy conditions must expend roughly two and a half times more effort to catch about a quarter fewer prey, while engine noise from ships disrupts communication and disorientates marine mammals such as whales and dolphins.

The consequences extend beyond individual animals. Many species simply abandon noisy places, which shrinks the area available to them and therefore caps the size of their populations; dolphins shun noisy coastlines, and bats have been observed avoiding roads that were loud during a motorcycle rally long after the vehicles had departed. Across the animals studied, mortality in noise-affected areas rose by 138 per cent on average, sometimes abruptly, as when underwater explosions rupture a fish's bladder, and sometimes slowly, through starvation caused by poorer hunting. Hansjoerg Kunc, one of the researchers, concedes that not every animal loses out, since certain predators gain temporarily when noise flushes prey from cover. He regards that as beside the point, however: an ecosystem whose species have adapted to one acoustic environment is being distorted by an external stressor, and he argues that conservation should protect the natural soundscape alongside physical habitat.

## C1 — One Gene, Many Questions (ns-c1-zfx-autism-v3)

Autism is diagnosed less often in women and girls, and researchers have now pointed to a single gene on the X chromosome, ZFX, as a possible contributor to that gap. Females carry two X chromosomes and, unusually for X-linked genes, express both copies of ZFX, so they produce more of it than males do; the gene appears to keep other autism-associated genes switched on when they might otherwise be silenced. The widely quoted claim that autism is up to four times as common in males is contested, partly because autism presents differently in females and because many girls and women mask their traits, which delays diagnosis or prevents it altogether. Nevertheless, many specialists accept the existence of a female protective effect, and women who are diagnosed tend to carry more autism-linked mutations than diagnosed men, implying that females need a heavier genetic load before the condition becomes apparent.

To investigate, Rebecca Andersen's team grew neural progenitor cells in culture and used a gene-editing tool to degrade the RNA from 20 genes known to be linked to autism. Reducing any one of these genes usually lowered the activity of most of the others, a degree of convergence the researchers said they had not expected. A model of the interactions then identified a core of 70 genes that strongly regulate the 20 and one another, and, among genes more active in female brains, pointed to ZFX. If ZFX acts as a genetic buffer, a girl could inherit many variants that reduce the expression of autism-associated genes and yet be protected because ZFX raises it again. An independent expert called the idea biologically plausible, but cautioned that it rests on the model, that nobody has demonstrated that boosting ZFX rescues the effects of such variants, and that a protective effect this complex is unlikely to trace back to one gene alone.

## C1 — A Hiss to Help the Sleeping Brain (ns-c1-pink-noise-v4)

Brief bursts of pink noise, a low-pitched hiss, played during sleep might give people with early-stage Alzheimer's disease a slight memory boost, according to a small study of 37 participants who had been diagnosed with the disease and had mild cognitive impairment affecting memory. There is some evidence that exposure to pink noise during sleep may help clear waste products from the brain, and the researchers reasoned that it could also support the consolidation of memories, a process that Alzheimer's can disrupt and that relies on slow brainwaves and short bursts of neural activity.

On two evenings fourteen days apart, the volunteers came to the laboratory, memorised 30 word pairs and slept there while their brain activity was monitored. On the first night, a system designed by a Spanish neurotechnology company detected when slow brainwaves were about to peak and triggered a 50-millisecond pulse of pink noise each time; on the second night no noise was played. The timing matters, according to team member Javier Minguez, because the useful window is only about 200 milliseconds wide. After each night the participants tried to recall the pairs, and they remembered 0.88 more pairs, on average, after the night with pink noise, which the researchers equate to an improvement of about 19 per cent. They stress, however, that the clinical significance remains uncertain, and they disclose that several authors have commercial links to the company or its spin-off and that three are inventors on a pending patent covering the method.

## C1 — A Blood Thinner for Painful Knees (ns-c1-osteoarthritis-v4)

A drug that prevents blood clots reduced pain and improved mobility in mice and monkeys with knee osteoarthritis, and the approach appears to work partly by dampening inflammatory signals that degrade cartilage. Osteoarthritis results from the wear and tear of the cartilage that cushions the ends of bones, which leads to inflammation, pain and stiffness; painkillers and anti-inflammatory drugs can ease the symptoms, but no approved drug halts or slows the worsening of the condition. Two inflammatory molecules, TNF-alpha and IL-1-beta, are widely thought to drive that inflammation, yet trials of drugs aimed at either one have failed, possibly because the molecules compensate for each other when one is blocked, according to Chuan-Ju Liu at Yale University and his colleagues.

To test whether targeting both would work better, the team surgically removed part of the cartilage in the left knees of eight monkeys; a month later four of them began to receive a daily dose of dabigatran, a drug commonly used to prevent clots in people at risk of stroke that also blocks signalling through both molecules. Seven months on, scans showed that the cartilage in the treated monkeys was about 60 per cent thicker on average, and their left knees had roughly 15 per cent more range of motion than those of the controls. Experiments in mice gave similar results. Outside experts were encouraging but careful: Tonia Vincent at the University of Oxford said a small clinical trial would be worth considering if another group replicated the findings, and she noted that some of the benefit might come from the drug's anti-clotting rather than its anti-inflammatory action. Because reducing clotting raises the risk of bleeding, Liu's team is now developing drugs that hit the two molecules without affecting clotting.

## C2 — Opening the Door Without the Key (ns-c2-technodelics-v4)

Psychedelics such as psilocybin, ketamine and LSD are increasingly regarded as remarkably effective treatments for serious mental illness, yet how they work remains unclear. Their benefit might reside in the radical disruption of brain chemistry and neural firing patterns, in the overwhelming experience of the trip, in the therapy that gives it meaning, or in some combination of the three. Most attempts to disentangle these possibilities have so far concentrated on engineering analogues that omit the trip yet, in mice, appear to reproduce some of the drugs' effects on neurons.

A growing minority of neuroscientists, among them Nicco Reggente at the University of California, Los Angeles, are exploring the converse hypothesis: that the therapeutic value derives from an intense alteration of perception, which upends a person's sense of self and of their place in the world. If so, drugs may be dispensable whenever other means can shake up the mental snow globe and let it settle into a healthier arrangement; psychedelics would merely be particularly good at picking the lock of a door that has other keys. Such alternatives have a long pedigree. Holotropic breathwork, a form of deliberate hyperventilation, was devised in the 1970s after psychedelic drugs were outlawed, and meditation and sensory deprivation can also transform mental states. Interest in these methods grew out of the psychedelic research of the 1950s and 1960s, but waned as Prozac and other antidepressants arrived and mental health research moved on.

With many obstacles to the clinical use of psychedelics unresolved, drug-free approaches have returned to the agenda, this time with technology as a central component. The best studied is stroboscopic light stimulation, in which rapidly flashing lights beamed onto closed eyelids generate vivid visual hallucinations within seconds, without the training that meditation or breathing practices require. In a 2024 study of 262 people, the technique reduced anxiety and improved mood as effectively as breath-focused meditation, and did so in just over five minutes rather than ten. One proposed explanation is predictive processing, the theory that the brain builds its picture of the self and the world by generating predictions from past experience and comparing them with incoming sensory information; colours that move in unexpected ways would then undermine the implicit confidence that the world is as it habitually appears, potentially opening the mind to change.

## C2 — A Clock Timed by the Sun (ns-c2-solar-clock-v4)

Optical clocks, the most precise timekeepers ever built, normally owe their accuracy to atoms isolated from the outside world: an electron inside an atom is made to oscillate between two energy levels, an ultra-stable laser is locked to the frequency of that oscillation, and a device called a frequency comb converts the rapid light waves into microwaves that ordinary electronics can count as ticks. Researchers led by Scott Diddams at the University of Colorado, Boulder, have now built a version in which the atomic reference is replaced by something that occurs at the sun itself.

Instead of locking their laser to an electron's jumps inside a laboratory atom, the team locked it to a process in which sunlight causes comparable jumps as it strikes iron atoms in the solar atmosphere. That process leaves a signature in the spectrum of colours that make up sunlight, so the researchers channelled sunlight into a special fibre and fed it into the rest of the apparatus. A clock of this kind has never been constructed before. It does not rival the best atomic optical clocks in accuracy, but during the partial solar eclipse of 2024 the team observed a characteristic shift in the spectrum as the moon passed in front of the sun, which suggests that such a device could serve as a tool for studying stars.

Fed with light from a distant star, the same design might indicate the presence of orbiting exoplanets through similar shifts, although starlight would have to be gathered by large telescopes, sometimes over long periods. Piet Schmidt, of the National Metrology Institute of Germany, welcomed the work because it draws on light from the stars rather than on objects kept under tight laboratory control, yet he cautioned that as an astronomical instrument it does not yet surpass existing methods, and that for very faint, distant stars its usefulness would be severely limited. He sees better prospects closer to home, where the clock might probe eruptions, sunspots and the solar wind in the outer layers of the sun's atmosphere.

- C) A clock referenced to the sun has replaced the frequency comb, because sunlight can be counted directly by ordinary electronics.
- D) A clock referenced to the sun is less accurate than laboratory clocks, but shifts in its sunlight suggest uses in astronomy.

D: İlk iki paragraf güneşteki bir sürece dayanan saati, üçüncü paragraf astronomik kullanımını ve en iyi olanağın Güneş'in incelenmesinde olduğunu anlatır.

## C2 — Economics, Not Summits (ns-c2-climate-economics-v3)

The hosts of the COP31 summit, Turkey and Australia, are urging a clean energy revolution while expanding fossil fuel production: Turkey has announced two new oilfields and a plan to double natural gas output, and Australia has approved projects that include a coal mine extension that critics call a climate bomb. Collective climate action has effectively stalled since the 2015 Paris Agreement, which nevertheless shaved more than 1C off the projected warming trajectory, and current policies still point towards a rise of about 2.7C by 2100, according to Climate Action Tracker. There is one encouraging sign: the Global Carbon Budget expects fossil fuel emissions to fall slightly in 2026, by 0.4 per cent, although that drop would not have occurred without the oil price spike caused by the Iran war.

The central argument is that economics, rather than diplomacy, is now driving the transition. Solar generates ten times more energy than when the Paris deal was signed, making it the fastest-growing source of electricity ever, and electricity from solar and wind costs less on average than electricity from gas or coal. Auke Hoekstra of Eindhoven University of Technology contends that policy was never the main driver, because the technologies are simply superior; Texas, the leading oil-producing state, has become the leading producer of solar and wind power, and in Nigeria and Pakistan households are installing cheap Chinese panels instead of waiting for governments to upgrade unreliable fossil-fuelled grids. Cheap batteries are solving the remaining drawback, that solar and wind produce little energy during evening peak demand.

Policy still matters, however, since early subsidies and regulations in China and Germany created the conditions for renewables to fall down the cost curve, and governments continue to provide more than 900 billion dollars a year in support for fossil fuels. Expanding the grid is the biggest bottleneck, something governments could speed up by cutting red tape and offering subsidies and loans. Kingsmill Bond of the think tank Ember Energy believes that the economics of clean technology will allow the world to limit warming to well below 2.7C, with the exact outcome depending on politics, and he proposes that energy-importing countries convene their own COP in which fossil fuel exporters cannot delay progress.
