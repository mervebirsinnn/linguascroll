## B1 — Before the First Message

Lena usually reads work messages while waiting for her morning bus. One Monday, her phone battery was empty, so she put the phone in her bag and looked around. She noticed a small garden beside the bus stop and watched a neighbour water the plants. She arrived at work at the usual time.

The next day, Lena charged her phone but kept it in her bag until she reached the office. She did not want to miss important messages, so she checked them before starting work. She still took the same bus, but the journey felt calmer. After a week, she decided to continue her new routine.

A: Bahçeyi fark etmesi yeni bir güzergâha geçtiğini göstermez; aynı otobüsü kullanır.

## B2 — A room for the unexpected visitor

Until recently, visitors to the Riverside Library could reserve a study room for an entire afternoon. The system suited regular users, who often booked several weeks ahead. However, people whose work schedules changed at short notice rarely found a room available. Several complained that a public service was becoming difficult to use unless they could plan their time far in advance.

The library tested a different arrangement for one month. Half the rooms remained bookable online, while the others could only be requested on arrival. Staff expected more complaints from regular users, but the response was mixed. Some disliked losing certainty; others welcomed the chance to make an unexpected visit. Meanwhile, a queue developed at the desk during the busiest hours.

The manager did not describe the trial as a complete success. More visitors had obtained a room, but staff were spending longer explaining the rules. She therefore kept both routes and introduced a clear sign showing which rooms were available. Rather than choosing between planning and flexibility, the library was trying to offer each without making the other impossible.

- B) To compare online booking with a proposed system of longer visits.
- C) To explain a change designed to reward the most frequent visitors.
- D) To assess whether a booking trial reduced the staff’s workload.

**Doğru cevap:** A

A: Yönetici iki yolu korur: önceden ayırtma ile plansız erişimi birlikte mümkün kılmaya çalışır.

C: Değişiklik düzenli kullanıcılara ödül vermek için yapılmaz; önceden planlayamayanların erişim sorununa yanıt verir.

D: Personelin açıklama yükü artar; iş yükünü azaltma metnin ana amacı değildir.

## C1 — Two meanings of learning better

To ask whether sleep improves learning is already to compress several questions into one. Evidence that a memory survives an interval need not show that the learner has become more receptive to information not yet encountered. Nor would identifying a biological change, however persuasive as an explanation of one task, establish its relevance to another. A 2014 investigation of mice illustrates the distinction: following movement training, sleeping animals developed more new connections on particular branches of brain cells than animals kept awake. What remains unsettled is not the observation itself, but the distance over which an explanation may legitimately travel.

A human study published in 2025 exposes a related difficulty without resolving it. Across two experiments, sleep favoured recall of previously learned word pairs, whereas fresh pairs were learned without a corresponding advantage. Planned analyses did not establish the anticipated relationship between retention and subsequent learning. A further analysis, which took earlier recall into account, did suggest a relationship. To privilege that finding simply because it supplies a coherent story would reverse the appropriate burden of explanation: a result obtained after reconsidering the analysis requires investigation, rather than retrospectively making the original comparisons dispensable.

It would nevertheless be premature to treat the missing advantage as evidence that preserving memories cannot facilitate later learning. That conclusion would require the particular tasks to exhaust the relevant possibilities, an assumption these findings do not warrant. The converse shortcut is no safer: to describe retention and acquisition as aspects of memory does not make improvement in one sufficient evidence of improvement in the other. The studies are therefore neither rivals from which a reader must select a winner nor interchangeable demonstrations of a general benefit. Their contribution is conditional: each constrains an account of learning, provided the account retains the distinctions that make its supporting observations meaningful.

## C2 — Confidence Without a Shortcut

When a disputed finding is presented to the public, the invitation to trust it can be hedged until responsibility for accepting it becomes difficult to locate. The National Academies report distinguishes reproducing an analysis with existing data and code from testing findings with newly collected data. Neither a successful repetition nor a failed one delivers an automatic verdict. The question for an explanation is consequently not whether uncertainty has been mentioned, but whether its implications have been made available for judgement.

Consider a hypothetical journal whose editors disagree over a contested result. One would postpone coverage until the objections had been resolved; another would publish immediately, attaching qualifications that readers could consult if they wished. Their disagreement appears to concern how much protection an audience requires. Yet the protection offered by each can obscure the same omission: neither proposal specifies what readers would need to understand in order to assess the conclusion. A warning held in reserve may be impeccably accurate while doing little to alter the confidence invited by the headline.

This is not an argument for giving every objection an equal hearing, as though fairness could be measured by the space allocated to opposing voices. An objection that makes a measurement unreliable has a different bearing on a conclusion from one that confines its application to a narrower setting. Were those differences explained, declining to distribute attention evenly might become more defensible, rather than less. Conversely, a meticulous account of limitations could still mislead if it left the strength of the remaining claim untouched by everything it had just conceded.

The demanding alternative is to make the relationship between confidence and its grounds inspectable, without promising that inspection will settle the dispute. Readers need not inherit the editor's judgement, but they should be able to identify what would justify revising it. Such an account asks authority to expose its dependence on reasons, including reasons that presently favour the claim. Its restraint consists neither in withholding a conclusion nor in surrounding one with reservations; it consists in refusing to let the conclusion outrun the explanation on which its credibility depends.
